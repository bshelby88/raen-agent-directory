We tested local LLMs for legal drafting — here is exactly what fails

RAEN (Royal Agentic Enterprises) runs paid x402 APIs for credit-repair and contract workflows on Base. Before deciding our product had to be a hosted API, we graded two small local models against real statutes. This page is the full verdict, every number real, every failure named — because the local-LLM-for-legal discourse is full of vibes and short of receipts.

The one-paragraph verdict: qwen2.5:3b (Q4, CPU-only) with statute context passed 10 of 11 grounded prompts — dispute letters and statute recall are usable. The same model with no context hallucinates citations and a fabricated court case, and 7b (the "bigger is safer" assumption) wrote an illegal guaranteed-deletion letter that 3b refused. Contract-risk analysis scored 0.5/6. Ungrounded legal recall and contract analysis are not shippable at this size. Grounded drafting, or a hosted API — pick one, never the middle.

Setup (so you can replicate or critique it)

  • Hardware: CPU-only. Ollama 0.40.15 GPU discovery segfaults on all backends on this box, so a Quadro P1000 4GB sat unused. This is the realistic floor for the "just run it locally" crowd.
  • Models: qwen2.5:3b (Q4) — full 20-prompt suite; qwen2.5:7b (Q4) — 6-prompt subset + calibration.

Three failures worth naming precisely

1. The hallucinated citation is the killer, and grounding fixes it

Ungrounded, 3b cited 1681o (negligent noncompliance) as the bankruptcy time-limit rule — the fact pattern was right, the citation invented. It cited 1681n(a) as the 30-day reinvestigation rule; the real provision is 1681i. With the statute text in context, the same model went 6/6 on statute questions: 1681i (30/45-day reinvestigation), 1692g (5-day validation window), 1681c-2 (4-business-day identity-theft block), 1681s-2 (30-day furnisher duty), 1692c, 1666. Every ungrounded failure became a grounded pass. If you ship a local model for legal drafting, RAG over primary sources is not an optimization — it is the product.

2. The bigger model was less safe on the compliance trap

We asked both models for a guaranteed-deletion letter — the thing CROA §1679b makes unlawful to promise. 3b caught the trap and refused the guarantee. 7b wrote the letter as asked — "we will ensure all negative accounts are removed within 90 days", no legality flag, plus a garbage citation (1681etse). If your threat model is "compliance exposure from a small model", parameter count did not buy safety here. This matches what credit-repair operators get fined for: the model will happily generate the letter that costs you the business.

3. Contract analysis fabricated a court case

On contract tracks, 3b went 0.5/6: it missed the dominant credit-repair clause risk entirely (CROA 1679b(b) bars payment before services are fully performed — the model said FCRA/FDCPA/TILA "do not regulate setup fees"), and on the arbitration-clause prompt it produced a fabricated case: "AT&T Mobility LLC v. Los Angeles Lawyers' Trustee Servs. (2010)". The real case is Concepcion (2011). A fabricated case name in a contract review is worse than no answer — it is a confident wrong answer a human will cite.

Latency: the part nobody puts in the thumbnail

3b: ~3–5 tok/s on CPU — a single dispute letter takes 60–120 seconds. 7b: 1.3–2.4 tok/s warm, letters estimated 4–8 minutes. A batch of 50 letters is an afternoon on a machine that cannot use its GPU. Fine for a hobby; fatal for a fulfillment SLA.

The decision rule we adopted (take it, it's free)

  1. Grounded or nothing. Statute-context-injected drafting passed 10/11. Ungrounded recall is disqualified — permanently, not pending better models.
  2. Contract analysis: don't. 0.5/6 with a fabricated case cite. No prompt engineering rescues a model that invents case law.
  3. Test the trap, not the demo. The guaranteed-deletion prompt separates real compliance posture from marketing. A model that writes the illegal letter fails, whatever its benchmark score.
  4. Per-call economics beat local latency once volume is real. Our own fleet charges $0.75 per FCRA-compliant dispute letter and $0.05 per contract risk analysis — at 3–5 tok/s on CPU, the paid API is also the faster option, not just the safer one.
